Learning System — plain-language overview
Overview
Overview — plain-language draft — v0.2

Three ways to hold what we learn, and two questions that keep the whole thing honest.

When something worth learning comes up, it gets handled in one of three ways: a step-by-step ladder for testing claims, a check against our shared values, or a practice built to protect things people aren't ready to put into words. A simple sorting step — not a manager, not a committee — decides which one fits. But none of those three tell us whether people are actually getting better at learning, or whether people feel safe enough to speak up honestly before they're forced to tidy their thinking into something official. This idea is our attempt to cover both sides: what we hold onto, and what makes it safe to hold.

What changed

Why not one system with add-ons

We previously sent material and early stage thinking that we could create a "base model" and attach modules to it. An add-on is never really equal to the main thing it's attached to, no matter how carefully it's designed. Treating refusal, silence, or gut feeling as optional extras quietly decided, before anyone got a say, that they mattered less than the main system. This is a change to how we handle content specifically — claims, values, and boundaries. A separate piece — how people grow as learners, and what conditions help them speak up honestly — didn't exist in either version. That's covered further down the page.

The old design

One system, with add-ons

One main ladder. Everything else — onboarding, blockers, legal work, refusal — attached to it as an optional extra.

The new design

Three equal approaches, sorted as they arrive

Three approaches now sit side by side as equals. A simple sorting step decides which one fits a new piece of information — none of the three outranks the others. But content isn't the whole story: the conditions around the team come first, and what people learn along the way feeds back into those conditions.

There's no single gatekeeper making this decision for everyone. Whoever holds the information chooses where it goes, right away, at no cost to them. What keeps this honest: every choice is written down straight away, for anyone to see, and anyone working in a different practice can challenge a routing decision — someone else just takes a fresh, independent look at it.

Having three practices instead of one makes room for every kind of knowledge a team produces — not just the kind that can be tested and proven. Each practice represents a different kind of commitment, and each one is open for others to see and question.

⌄ Knowledge ladder a guess → a firm, tested commitment
Five steps, checked more carefully as the stakes go up: guess → hunch → educated guess → tested claim → solid, well-tested claim. Only a solid claim can justify spending real time or money on something. Everything learned along the way stays on record, so any action taken can always be traced back to what justified it.

The ladder makes it clear and official when a belief has earned the right to be acted on — from a first guess through to a well-tested claim.

↻ what's learned feeds back in as a new, better-informed starting point
→ we still haven't worked out how to properly credit whoever first raised it — see the open questions
⌄ Principles fidelity no steps, no ranking
A small, steady set of principles that the whole team is accountable to, checked all the time against what's actually happening — not "is this claim proven," but "are we still doing what we said we'd do." This check can send any piece of work — even something already on the ladder — back to sorting if something doesn't add up.

This practice makes it clear whether we're keeping our word — not just at the end of a project, but continuously, all the way through.

↻ can send things back to sorting at any time
never escalates, and never produces a commitment of its own
⌄ Relational practice paying close attention, protecting the right to say no
This is about paying close attention rather than jumping to conclusions. Staying silent, leaving something unsaid, or refusing to fully explain something are all treated as valid outcomes here — not as a failure to make progress. Nothing here has to feed into the ladder. The only way something moves into a different practice is if the person holding it agrees to it.

This practice makes it fully acceptable to say no — a boundary, once set, is protected and recognised, not something someone has to do quietly because there's nowhere else for it to go.

nothing is forced back into the system — it stays held, or refused, entirely on the person's own terms
Learning as Practice

There's more to learning than what we know

The three practices above all deal with content — a claim, a value, or a boundary. None of them tell us whether the people involved are actually getting better at learning, or whether the right conditions exist for someone to speak up honestly before they feel forced to package it neatly. These aren't a fourth practice, and they don't produce any kind of formal commitment. They're ongoing questions that the whole system has to keep answering, across all three practices.

What kind of learners are we becoming
learning how to learn, not just learning facts

The ladder tracks how well-tested a claim is. It has no way of tracking whether the person raising claims is changing — for example, whether someone who used to push every hunch straight to the ladder now sends it to the relational practice instead, without being told to, or becomes more willing to raise an uncomfortable hunch in the first place.

There's a useful difference between learning to do something better, and learning whether you're even asking the right kind of question in the first place. The fact that sorting decisions get written down and can be challenged is already a small example of that second, deeper kind of learning — questioning the system's own habits, not just its results. It's worth asking whether that kind of self-questioning should be something we can actually see and track in people, not only in the written record.

a change in how someone behaves, not just what's written down
The conditions for learning to happen at all
feeling safe · having spare time · regular pauses · many viewpoints

This comes back to something raised earlier: telling the truth about what isn't working has to cost people nothing real, or the whole idea of letting people sort their own information falls apart. Research on psychological safety backs this up directly — teams speak up more when doing so isn't held against them.

Feeling safe isn't the whole picture, though. Having spare time and attention matters too, so people can notice a weak signal before they're forced to tidy it into something official. So do regular moments set aside just for noticing things, not tied to a deadline or a decision. And so does having more than one point of view in the room — several partial perspectives tend to beat one confident, single view, which is exactly what happened when three separate outside reviewers, working independently, landed on the same core criticism of this whole design.

the groundwork the whole system depends on — not a fourth practice
How the pieces move

How these fit together, and how you'd bring this into a team

Conditions and content aren't equals. Conditions come first — they decide whether something worth learning ever gets raised honestly at all. Content is the visible, day-to-day middle. Learners is the knock-on effect: practising this well, over time, changes people, and those changed people go on to protect and strengthen the very conditions that made honest practice possible in the first place.

A rough order for bringing this into a team or organisation — each step assumes the ones before it are real, not just announced:

01

Check the conditions before touching anything else

Before introducing a ladder or any practice: has raising an inconvenient finding ever actually cost someone here their standing? Does anyone have time in their week that isn't tied to a deadline? Do decisions draw on more than one point of view, or mainly one confident voice?

02

Make any governance changes real, not just declared

Clear decision-making rights, real budget for the two practices that won't produce neat, fundable-looking outputs, and a genuine commitment that telling the truth won't be punished — tested by whether it actually holds the first time someone puts it to the test, not by the statement alone.

03

Try it with one small team first, not the whole organisation

The basic loop — something comes up → sorting → three practices — works the same way at any size. One team doing it properly is a better, more honest test than a half-hearted launch across the whole organisation at once.

04

Treat "learners" as a light, protected habit — not something you measure

A regular space where people notice, together, how their own thinking is shifting. The moment this turns into a tracked, reviewed measure, it becomes exactly the kind of box-ticking this whole approach is trying to avoid. It should feel more like the values check — ongoing and low-key — than like the ladder.

05

Only then make the content side official

The written sorting log, the right to challenge a decision, the three practices themselves — by this point, people have real reason to trust that using the values check or the relational practice costs them nothing.

06

Pay closest attention to fairness-checking and credit-giving

These are the two places where conditions and content actually meet in practice — checking whether some people are being routed differently because of existing power or lack of spare time, using what the sorting log actually shows.

07

Apply this approach to its own rollout

Pushback, an unused practice, or people quietly slipping back into old habits while this is being introduced is itself something worth learning from — sort it the same way you'd sort anything else, rather than treating the rollout as separate from the thing being rolled out.

How this goes wrong

Introducing the content side and the learners side without first getting the conditions right repeats a mistake that's easy to make: building the safety net second, if at all. A full three-practice system with weak conditions underneath doesn't fail with a bang — it just quietly drifts back toward whichever practice feels safest, which in practice usually means everyone defaults back to the ladder, and the other two practices become vocabulary nobody actually uses.

Where this builds on earlier work

This isn't a new discovery

None of this came out of nowhere. Eight "deep codes" from an earlier field guide named most of these same tensions before the knowledge ladder even existed. Two of them — Holistic view and Mindful endings — were quietly lost along the way when the ladder was first built, without anyone actually deciding to drop them.

Unfurling

The shape of the work reveals itself over time — it isn't fixed at the start.

→ the ladder can revise what "done" means, and loops back on itself
Balanced power

Authority shouldn't depend on who has the most wealth, seniority, or reputation.

→ people sort their own information · scrutiny scales with the stakes, not with rank
Holistic view

What matters includes relationships, care, and craft — not just what's easy to measure.

→ the ladder's own admitted blind spot around unspoken knowledge · the reason the relational practice exists
Mindful endings

People should be able to step back responsibly, without stalling the group's mission.

→ saying no is treated as a legitimate outcome, not a failure to make progress
Mission led

Being accountable to the shared mission matters more than individual interest.

→ what counts as "done" · credibility is earned through the work itself, not through position
Risk mitigation

Reducing the chance and impact of things going wrong, for everyone involved.

→ tracking real-world risk alongside the ladder as it develops
Risk ownership

Financial and relational risk should be shared visibly and fairly.

→ the bigger question of properly crediting whoever first raised something
Proportionate

How closely something is checked should scale with context and complexity — not be fixed in advance.

→ scrutiny that scales with the stakes · the whole argument against one-system-with-add-ons
Being honest about this

What we haven't solved yet

Naming these plainly rather than glossing over them — this is still closer to a working idea than a finished, tested design.

01

Letting people sort their own information is only as safe as reward structures nobody has actually designed yet. Someone in a precarious position, or answerable to a funder, may not experience "just hold this quietly" as a real option, even when it's technically allowed. We've named what would need to be true — people feeling safe, having spare time, regular pauses, many viewpoints — but not yet worked out how an organisation actually builds those things.

02

The ladder still doesn't fit messy, tangled problems well. Making it one of three practices stops it pretending to be the whole answer — but it doesn't fix the deeper mismatch between "prove it with a clean test" thinking and problems where cause and effect aren't straightforward.

03

We have a principle for giving credit back, but no actual mechanism. What should actually go back to whoever first raised something — credit, resources, a say in what happens next — hasn't been designed.

04

Not having enough qualified reviewers is still the bottleneck. Sorting things more carefully doesn't create more people qualified to review a claim at the top of the ladder.

05

"Learners" and "conditions" have been named, but not actually built. Same gap as the credit problem above, one level up: there's no real way yet to check how healthy the conditions are, or to notice how someone's thinking is shifting, except informally. Describing the idea isn't the same as building it.

06

The suggested order assumes a pause that most organisations can't actually take. "Fix the conditions before touching anything else" is the honest order to follow — but most teams are already mid-project, already answerable to funders expecting ladder-shaped results on a schedule. What to do when that pause simply isn't available is still an open question, and that's probably the normal case, not the exception.
